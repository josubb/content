Why large prawns make potted shrimps rubbery

British seafood | prawn recipes | cold appetisers

Source: https://cheeserealm.com/why-large-prawns-make-potted-shrimps-rubbery/
Potted shrimps depend on small, tender prawns that absorb spiced butter without losing their delicate bite. Large cooked gambas release less flavour into the butter and turn rubbery when reheated.

Potted shrimps are small cooked shrimp preserved under spiced butter, traditionally made with Morecambe Bay brown shrimp and served cool with hot toast.

What potted shrimps are and how to serve them

Potted shrimps are a chilled British shellfish spread made from very small shrimp, butter, mace and a little cayenne pepper.

Brown shrimp are small, soft and naturally sweet, so butter coats them like sauce coating short pasta. Large prawns behave differently: their fibres are longer and firmer, so the mixture can feel dry even when there is plenty of butter.

Serve them cool, not fridge-hard

Serve a 100 to 125 g pot after 10 to 15 minutes at room temperature, then spread it over hot toast. The toast should be hot enough to soften the top butter, but not so hot that the shrimp cook again.

Morecambe Bay, on England’s north-west coast, is the place most closely associated with classic potted shrimps. Its small brown shrimp have a sweet, concentrated shellfish flavour and a delicate texture that suits spiced butter better than large prawns. The traditional style uses the shrimp whole or nearly whole, with mace seasoning and cayenne pepper kept restrained so the seafood remains the main flavour.

Outside the UK, the exact shrimp can be difficult to source, but small local shrimp are a better substitute than oversized prawns because they give the same spoonable, buttery texture.

In Spain, small shrimp beat large cooked prawns

Small peeled shrimp make the closest Spanish version because they stay tender during brief warming and hold butter evenly.
