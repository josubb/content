A reported attack on UK power infrastructure highlights how quickly a disruption can extend beyond the primary target and affect dependent SMEs. Resilience planning and cyber insurance should account for supplier outages, operational interruption, and the cost of restoring critical services.

A power-sector cyber incident is not only an infrastructure issue

MediaNama’s report on an alleged Iranian cyberattack against a UK power plant should be read carefully by UK small and medium-sized enterprises. The immediate target may have been critical national infrastructure, but the commercial consequences of a successful disruption would extend far beyond the operator of the facility.

For an SME, a loss of electricity, telecommunications, payment connectivity or access to cloud-based systems can stop trading within minutes. A café may be unable to process card payments or keep refrigerated stock safe. A manufacturer may lose production time, damage work in progress and miss contractual delivery dates. A professional-services firm may be unable to access client files, conduct remote meetings or operate its phone system.

The important lesson is not that every small business is likely to be directly targeted by a state-linked actor. It is that cyber risk can become a dependency risk: an attack elsewhere can interrupt the suppliers and infrastructure an SME relies on every day.

What this report means for UK SME risk planning

The report’s framing links the incident to geopolitical cyber activity. Public attribution in cyber incidents can evolve, and businesses should avoid drawing operational conclusions from headlines alone. Nevertheless, the scenario is credible enough to test an uncomfortable assumption: that electricity, internet access, banking, cloud platforms and key software suppliers will always be available.

A conventional cyber incident at a small business often starts with phishing, compromised Microsoft 365 credentials, an exposed remote-access service or ransomware.
