Oxford Street’s charging decision is about more than four parking spaces

The reported loss of four of the 22 disabled bays at the car park beside Swansea’s Grand Theatre for electric-vehicle (EV) chargers has created a legitimate local tension. Swansea needs dependable public charging if drivers are to move away from petrol and diesel cars. But accessible parking is not a surplus convenience that can simply be repurposed when new infrastructure arrives.

For people who rely on a Blue Badge bay, the location, width, kerb-free route, lighting and proximity to a destination can determine whether a visit to the city centre is possible at all. Reducing provision from 22 to 18 bays is an 18% reduction at that particular car park. The practical impact may be greater than that percentage suggests during theatre performances, shopping peaks, bad weather or medical appointments, when accessible bays are already in high demand.

The bigger lesson for Swansea is clear: EV infrastructure and accessibility must be designed together, not treated as competing afterthoughts. That matters to drivers, local businesses, property managers and electricians responsible for specifying and installing charging equipment.

Why public EV charging requires an accessibility-first design

A charging bay is not automatically accessible merely because it is on level ground. A driver with reduced mobility may need room to open a door fully, deploy a wheelchair, use a walking aid, manage a cable or reach the charger controls. Some EV charging leads are heavy and awkward, especially when a vehicle’s charge port is on the opposite side from the unit.

The difference between a disabled bay and an accessible charger

A standard disabled parking bay and an EV charging bay address different needs. A disabled bay typically prioritises extra transfer space and a safe pedestrian route.
