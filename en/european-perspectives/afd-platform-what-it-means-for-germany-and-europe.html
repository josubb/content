The AfD’s rise reflects growing pressure on Germany’s political consensus, particularly around migration, economic security, and national sovereignty. Its platform combines electoral appeal with proposals that could reshape Germany’s role in the European Union.

Why an AfD reference profile matters now

Encyclopedia Britannica’s updated overview of Alternative for Germany (AfD) is not a breaking-news report in the conventional sense. It is more useful than that: it consolidates the party’s beliefs, platform, election record and controversies into a reference point at a moment when AfD is no longer a marginal protest vehicle. For readers following a European alternative to the established political centre, the key question is not simply what AfD says. It is whether the party can turn electoral dissatisfaction into durable political power—and what institutional, economic and diplomatic costs might follow.

AfD’s significance comes from its ability to connect several grievances that are often discussed separately: irregular migration, pressure on public housing and local services, energy prices, industrial competitiveness, distrust of national media and political institutions, and concern about the transfer of decision-making to Brussels. Its voters are not a uniform bloc. Some are committed national conservatives; others are former non-voters or former supporters of centre-right, centre-left and eastern German regional parties. Treating all AfD support as identical obscures why it has proved resilient.

From euro crisis party to broad anti-establishment force

The party’s evolution explains its current coalition of voters

AfD was founded in 2013 amid opposition to eurozone bailouts. Its early identity was rooted in economic and monetary criticism: the argument that German taxpayers and savers were being exposed to risks created elsewhere in the euro area. That origin still matters, particularly for voters sceptical of EU fiscal integration.

Yet the party’s centre of gravity shifted substantially during and after the 2015 refugee crisis. Immigration, border control, asylum policy and German national identity became central to its public appeal.
