Beautiful, sustainable spaces cannot be truly transformative if they remain inaccessible to the people who live in them. The New European Bauhaus must connect design quality with tenant power, permanently affordable housing, and low-carbon construction standards.

The central tension: a green vision built on unequal ground

The New European Bauhaus (NEB) is one of the European Union’s most appealing ideas: make the green transition beautiful, sustainable and inclusive rather than merely technical. It promises buildings, public spaces and neighbourhoods that reduce emissions while improving everyday life. That is a necessary ambition. Europeans experience climate policy through their homes, streets, energy bills, transport routes and access to shade—not through policy slogans.

Yet the critique suggested by The Yale Review of International Studies article, “Green Dreams, Concrete Walls: The New European Bauhaus and the Fight Against Its Foundations,” goes to the heart of whether this initiative can deliver. A programme can fund elegant retrofits, innovative materials and photogenic public spaces while leaving the economic structures that produce housing insecurity, exclusion and high-carbon construction largely untouched.

For readers interested in a genuine European alternative, this is the decisive question: is the NEB changing how Europe looks, or changing who has power over how Europe is built? The first outcome has value. The second is what determines whether the green transition earns durable public legitimacy.

Why architecture cannot be separated from housing politics

Buildings are central to Europe’s climate challenge. The built environment accounts for a substantial share of energy use and emissions, both through operating buildings—heating, cooling and electricity—and through the materials used to construct them. Cement, steel, glass and demolition are not neutral background details. They lock in carbon emissions and financial choices for decades.

But housing is also where social inequality is most tangible. A homeowner may be able to access a grant for insulation, solar panels or a heat pump.
