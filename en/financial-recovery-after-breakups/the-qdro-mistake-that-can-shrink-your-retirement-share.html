Dividing a retirement account after divorce requires precision, because a single drafting error can alter the distribution, timing, and tax treatment of the benefit. A QDRO that looks correct on paper may still reduce the final share if its terms are not aligned with the plan document and settlement intent.

A retirement account can look simple on paper and still shrink fast in a divorce. The wrong valuation date, a missed tax issue, or a missing court order can turn a fair settlement into an expensive mistake, especially when a pension, 401(k), IRA, or annuity is involved.

Dividing retirement accounts in a divorce is not as simple as splitting the balance in half. Pensions, 401(k)s, IRAs, and annuities are handled differently, and the valuation date, tax rules, and court orders can change the final outcome. The right process protects both spouses and helps prevent costly errors.

First, know which account needs a QDRO

Employer-sponsored plans usually need a Qualified Domestic Relations Order, or QDRO, before the plan will pay a spouse’s share. IRAs usually do not need a QDRO and often move by a transfer incident to divorce instead. That difference sounds small, but it is where many people lose time and money.

A QDRO is a court order that tells a pension plan or 401(k) how to pay a former spouse. It must fit the plan’s own rules and ERISA requirements. The Department of Labor explains that a domestic relations order becomes “qualified” only when the plan administrator accepts it under QDRO rules from the Department of Labor.

401s and pensions need court orders

A 401(k) split usually needs a QDRO because the plan cannot safely send money to an ex-spouse without one. A defined benefit pension, which promises a monthly benefit later, also usually needs a QDRO before the plan will carve out the alternate payee’s share.

The error most people make here is assuming the divorce decree alone is enough. It is not.
