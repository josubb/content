Rats exploit spilled seed, sheltered corners, and easy access around bird feeders. Lasting control depends on removing food sources, improving feeder hygiene, and proofing vulnerable areas rather than relying on cheap deterrent gadgets.

A Daily Express report suggests that sprinkling a cheap household ingredient over the lawn can stop rats invading gardens. At 35p, the proposed fix is understandably appealing: it is quick, familiar and far less daunting than dealing with a suspected rat run beneath a shed or behind a compost bin.

For people who feed garden birds, however, the important question is not whether a strong-smelling ingredient might make one patch of lawn temporarily unpleasant for a rat. It is whether it removes the reasons rats are visiting in the first place. Usually, it does not.

Rats are resourceful wild animals. Where a garden offers regular food, water and cover, a scent-based deterrent is unlikely to provide a dependable long-term answer. A better response protects birds, pets and other wildlife while making the feeding area substantially less rewarding for rodents.

Why bird-feeding gardens can attract rats

Brown rats are widespread in UK towns, suburbs and rural areas. Seeing one does not automatically mean a home is unhygienic, nor does it mean bird feeding must stop forever. Rats travel through connected gardens, drainage routes, hedges and outbuildings, often unnoticed until a reliable food source appears.

Bird feeders can become that source when seed falls freely to the ground. High-energy mixes containing sunflower hearts, peanuts, maize and suet are as useful to rats as they are to birds. A feeder that is filled generously but spills daily can effectively create a predictable feeding station after dark.

The issue is compounded by several common garden features:

- open compost bins containing cooked-food scraps; - fallen fruit left beneath trees; - leaking water butts, dripping taps or pet-water bowls left overnight; - dense planting, timber stacks or clutter that provides cover; - bags of bird food stored in sheds without rodent-proof containers; - a feeder placed close to a fence, shed, hedge or low wall that offers easy access.
