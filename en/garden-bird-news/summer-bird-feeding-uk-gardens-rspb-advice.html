Summer Bird Feeding: What UK Gardens Should Do

Summer feeding can support UK garden birds during demanding breeding and moulting periods, but unsuitable food and poor hygiene can quickly create avoidable risks. A measured approach—clean feeders, fresh water, and seasonally appropriate foods—helps protect birds without disrupting natural foraging.

The summer-feeding debate is really about risk management

A reported disagreement between a bird-food company and RSPB advice on pausing summer feeding raises a useful question for UK householders: should a feeder stay up when the weather is warm?

The important point is that this is not simply a choice between being ‘for’ or ‘against’ feeding garden birds. Supplementary food can help birds at certain times, particularly where natural food is temporarily scarce. However, a feeder also brings many birds into close contact at one small, repeatedly used site. In warm weather, spilled seed, damp food, droppings and unwashed feeding equipment can create conditions in which disease is more easily transmitted.

For readers, the sensible response is neither panic nor blind routine. It is to treat feeding as a managed wildlife activity: assess the demand in the garden, use appropriate foods, keep equipment clean, and stop immediately if there are signs of illness.

Why summer is different from winter

Winter feeding is often discussed because cold weather, frozen ground and shorter days can reduce access to natural foods. Summer has different pressures. Many adult birds are feeding young, moulting after breeding, or recovering from the energy demands of nesting. Insects, caterpillars, fruits and other natural foods may be plentiful, depending on the habitat and weather.

That matters because many nestlings need protein-rich invertebrates rather than a diet based on dry seed. Parents may visit feeders for themselves, but a feeder should not be regarded as a replacement for the insects that chicks require. A garden with pesticide-free planting, native hedging, a pond or damp area, and a little untidy vegetation can be a more valuable summer resource than an additional sack of mixed seed.

Warm conditions also increase the practical hygiene burden.
