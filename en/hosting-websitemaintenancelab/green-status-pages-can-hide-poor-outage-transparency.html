Green Status Pages Can Hide Poor Outage Transparency

A fully green status page can look reassuring, yet it may conceal delayed updates, vague incident records, or incomplete outage histories. Evaluating transparency—not just current uptime—helps reveal the operational risk behind a hosting renewal.

A green status page shows only what a provider claims today. Before buying, migrating, or renewing, inspect its incident archive for response times, scope, updates, fixes, and postmortems.

Uptime percentages lack this context. The real risk is how the provider acts when systems fail.

How to judge a status page before buying

Judge the incident archive, not today’s color. A reliable provider shows when an event began and when it was acknowledged.

It should name affected components or regions. It should also show regular updates and a closing explanation.

Alan Curtis, Host Compare’s hosting reviewer, checks these records alongside uptime monitoring. Together, they show provider behavior under stress.

Check first notice and update cadence

A useful target for the first public update is 10 to 15 minutes after confirmed customer impact. Start the clock when the team knows service is affected.

Do not wait until root-cause work ends. “Investigating” followed by 90 minutes of silence says very little.

Fast notice matters during customer-facing failures.

Read component history, not headline uptime

Review six to twelve months of resolved events. Repeated network latency, packet loss, CDN, API, or regional cloud events matter most.

Check whether the provider names the affected scope. Check whether failover protected customers.

Compare these signals: First notice within 10 to 15 minutes. Promised updates every 30 to 60 minutes. Clear affected components. A postmortem should name corrective work.
