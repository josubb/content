US-East Often Beats US-West for Mixed Team Latency

For distributed teams, latency is not just a metric; it shapes how quickly people can collaborate without friction. Region choice affects response time, routing efficiency, and overall cost stability.

Picking the “closest” AWS region often misses the real bottleneck: mixed teams rarely experience latency the same way from every office, home network, or cloud dependency. A stack that feels fast to a developer in Chicago can feel sluggish to an operator in Austin or a customer in California, especially when control-plane traffic, databases, and APIs all route differently.

For distributed teams, the best region is not always the closest one: choose the location that gives the lowest real-world latency to the majority of users and operators, while balancing cost, resilience, and compliance. In many U.S. cases, us-east-1 offers the broadest connectivity, but us-east-2 can be a smarter default when pricing, capacity, and operational simplicity matter.

Which region is actually faster?

Low-latency vs US-West hosting for distributed teams is not a map problem. It is a routing problem with a geography layer on top.

East vs west in real use

US-East often wins for mixed U.S. teams because many networks reach Virginia and Ohio with cleaner paths. US-West often wins for West Coast-heavy groups, especially when the app sits near users in California, Oregon, or Washington.

The first surprise is simple: the closest region does not always answer first. A route that crosses fewer bad network hops can beat a shorter path on paper.

A 20 ms ping gap sounds small. In a dashboard, chat app, or API used all day, that gap stacks up fast.

Why nearest is not always fastest

Distance only sets the stage. Peering decides who hands traffic to whom, and routing decides the path in the middle.

A team in Texas can sometimes see better response times from Ohio than from Northern California. That happens when carriers have a cleaner path to the East Coast.
